3. How national policy shapes planting

How did the food security policy of the country influence the cultivation of corn?

China treats grain self-sufficiency as a national priority, with a 2026 target of about 700 million tonnes (USDA FAS 2026a).

Testing my trade policy chain

My progress report said Heilongjiang cut its corn area by three per cent in 2025 because lower imports led to poor prices. I took the three per cent from a USDA forecast (USDA FAS 2025), and its seven million tonne import forecast is now five million (USDA FAS 2026b). The province’s final statistics show that corn area actually rose 0.5 per cent and output rose 3.3 per cent, while soybean area fell 2.1 per cent (HPBS 2025, 2026).

Imports peaked at 29.5 million tonnes in the 2020 trade year and stayed high while China’s own harvests kept growing. This oversupply seems to have caused the weak prices after the 2024 harvest, and Beijing then restricted imports, which fell to 1.8 million tonnes (USDA FAS 2025, 2026b). So the weak prices led to the import cut, which is the reverse of my chain.

Prices then recovered, but slowly. By early 2026 they were 7 per cent higher than a year before and north-eastern corn farmers were making about US$436 per hectare (USDA FAS 2026a), so USDA now expects more corn in 2026 (USDA FAS 2026b). Imports affect planting with a delay, because farmers plant according to last season’s prices.

North-eastern soybean growers get RMB 300 to 500 per mu (US$654 to 1,090 per hectare) more in subsidies than corn growers (USDA FAS 2026a). That is more than a hectare of corn earns, yet soybean area still fell, so without the extra payment soybeans would probably lose even more land.

USDA writes for American farmers, who had lost their biggest corn buyer, which may explain why it focused on imports.
