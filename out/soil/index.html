2. How the black soil is being worn away

What geographical processes are deteriorating the black earth soils, and which agricultural practices contribute to these processes?

On the Songnen Plain the black topsoil was 60 to 80 centimetres thick in the 1950s and is now 20 to 40 (Liao et al. 2026). It is still thinning by 2 to 5 millimetres a year, and the subsoil of degraded fields could be fully exposed within 30 years (Jiang et al. 2025).

Three processes

Jiang et al. (2025) blame intensive tillage combined with wind, water and freeze-thaw erosion. Summer storms run off long, gentle slopes, and land sloping 3 to 10 degrees makes up 46.39 per cent of the eroded area (Sui and Dai 2025). Runoff follows the furrows, cutting rills and then gullies, and in spring the wind strips the bare fields.

Ploughing also lets air into the soil, so organic matter breaks down faster than roots and straw replace it, especially when straw is removed. In the top 5 centimetres, soil organic carbon has fallen from 44.7 to 23.9 grams per kilogram (Jiang et al. 2025), and soil with less organic matter erodes more easily.

Heavy machinery also compacts the soil (Liao et al. 2026). After 80 years of cultivation, the bulk density of the top 30 centimetres has risen 59.49 per cent and water holding capacity has fallen 53.90 per cent (Sui and Dai 2025), so less rain soaks in and more runs off.

FAO (2022) lists the same threats in all four black soil regions of the world, and Liao et al. (2026) describe comparable losses in Iowa and Ukraine, so the cause seems to be the farming system itself.
